​Technical Implementation Report:​
​Extending Neural Network Estimation​
​Frameworks to Two-Asset​
​Heterogeneous Agent New Keynesian​
​Models​

​1. Introduction and Theoretical Motivation​
​The quantitative analysis of macroeconomic policy has undergone a paradigm shift with the​
​ascendancy of Heterogeneous Agent New Keynesian (HANK) models. These frameworks have​
​fundamentally altered the understanding of monetary transmission by highlighting the role of​
​the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) and the distribution of wealth. However, a critical​
​dissonance persists between the theoretical richness of these models and the computational​
​feasibility of estimating them on aggregate time series data. The standard One-Asset HANK​
​framework, while tractable, fails to simultaneously replicate the high aggregate​
​wealth-to-income ratios and the high average MPCs observed in microeconomic data.​​1​ ​To​
​reconcile these empirical realities, the literature has gravitated toward Two-Asset models that​
​distinguish between liquid assets (cash, bonds) and illiquid assets (housing, equity), governed​
​by transaction costs.​
​This report delineates a comprehensive strategy to extend the neural network estimation​
​framework developed by Kase, Melosi, and Rottner (KMR, 2025) to accommodate the​
​structural complexities of a Two-Asset HANK environment. The KMR framework represents a​
​methodological breakthrough by treating structural parameters as pseudo-state variables​
​within a deep learning architecture, thereby allowing for a "solve once, estimate many times"​
​approach that circumvents the computational bottlenecks of traditional nested-loop​
​estimation methods.​​1​ ​The integration of the Two-Asset​​physics into this framework requires a​
​rigorous synthesis of the estimation rigor of KMR, the portfolio choice mechanics of Kaplan,​
​Moll, and Violante (2018), and the advanced deep learning techniques for high-dimensional​

​functional approximations pioneered by Han et al. (2021) and Maliar et al. (2021).​
​The primary objective of this implementation is to construct an estimator capable of​
​identifying the structural frictions—specifically portfolio adjustment costs and idiosyncratic​
​risk—that drive the "flight to liquidity" and capital premia during aggregate uncertainty​
​shocks.​​1​ ​By leveraging the scalability of neural networks,​​this approach aims to overcome the​
​"curse of dimensionality" inherent in tracking the joint distribution of liquid and illiquid wealth,​
​providing a robust tool for analyzing the interaction between inequality, asset markets, and​
​stabilization policy.​

​2. The Computational Architecture: The KMR​
​Framework​
​The foundational "blueprint" for this extension is the methodology proposed by Kase, Melosi,​
​and Rottner (2025), which fundamentally restructures the problem of estimating nonlinear​
​dynamic models. In standard Bayesian estimation, such as the Random Walk​
​Metropolis-Hastings (RWMH) algorithm, the structural model must be solved for equilibrium​
​dynamics at every iteration of the parameter chain. For heterogeneous agent models, where​
​solving for the equilibrium involves calculating the evolution of an infinite-dimensional​
​distribution, this repeated solution is computationally prohibitive.​

​2.1 The Pseudo-State Variable Mechanism​

​The central innovation of the KMR approach is the expansion of the state space to include​
​structural parameters. In a conventional recursive equilibrium, the policy function $\psi$ maps​
​the economic state variables $\mathbb{S}_t$ to control variables $C_t$, conditional on a fixed​
​vector of structural parameters $\Theta$:​

​$$C_t = \psi(\mathbb{S}_t; \Theta)$$​
​KMR reformulates this by treating the parameters $\Theta$ not as fixed constants during the​
​solution phase, but as inputs to the approximating neural network, effectively creating a​
​"global" solution across the parameter space.​​1​ ​The​​neural network $\psi_{NN}$ is trained to​
​approximate the policy function:​

​$$\psi_{NN}(\mathbb{S}_t, \Theta | \mathbf{W}) \approx \psi(\mathbb{S}_t)$$​
​where $\mathbf{W}$ represents the optimized weights and biases of the network. This​
​reformulation implies that the computationally intensive "solution step"—training the neural​
​network to satisfy the model's equilibrium conditions—occurs only once. During the​
​estimation phase, evaluating the likelihood of a new parameter candidate $\Theta_{new}$​
​drawn from the prior requires only a forward pass through the pre-trained network, which is a​
​computationally trivial matrix-vector multiplication operation.​
​This mechanism is particularly advantageous for HANK models because it decouples the​
​complexity of the model solution from the sample size of the estimation chain. However,​
​applying this to a Two-Asset model requires a precise definition of the augmented input​
​vector. The neural network must learn the manifold of solutions not just over the domain of​
​aggregate shocks (e.g., TFP or monetary policy shocks), but also over the domain of​
​structural frictions, such as the portfolio adjustment cost parameter $\chi$ and the elasticity​
​of substitution between assets.​

​2.2 Architecture of the Neural Network Particle Filter​

​Beyond solving the equilibrium policy functions, the KMR framework introduces a second​
​neural network designed specifically to approximate the likelihood function itself.​​1​ ​The​
​likelihood of a nonlinear DSGE model is typically estimated using a particle filter, a Monte​
​Carlo method that is itself noisy and computationally expensive. KMR proposes training a​
​"Neural Network Particle Filter" (NNPF) that maps the structural parameters directly to the​
​likelihood value.​
​The construction of the NNPF involves a three-stage process that must be adapted for the​
​Two-Asset environment:​
​1.​ ​Training Data Generation:​​A training set is constructed​​by drawing $N$ parameter​
​vectors $\{\Theta_i\}_{i=1}^N$ from the prior distributions. For each $\Theta_i$, the model​
​is simulated using the pre-trained policy network $\psi_{NN}$, and the likelihood​
​$\mathcal{L}(\Theta_i | Y_{data})$ is estimated using a standard particle filter.​
​2.​ ​Likelihood Function Approximation:​​A second neural​​network is trained to regress the​
​estimated likelihoods on the parameter vectors. This network acts as a global smoother,​
​filtering out the Monte Carlo noise inherent in the particle filter evaluations.​
​3.​ ​Posterior Sampling:​​The actual Bayesian estimation​​is performed by sampling from the​
​posterior using the likelihoods predicted by the NNPF.​
​This architecture is robust to the "ZLB instability" often encountered in HANK estimations.​​1​
​Acharya et al. (2023) note that particle filters often degenerate when the economy hits the​
​Zero Lower Bound (ZLB) for extended periods, as the likelihood surface becomes flat or​

​discontinuous. The NNPF smooths over these discontinuities, providing a stable gradient for​
​the estimator. For the Two-Asset model, this stability is crucial, as the "flight to liquidity"​
​during uncertainty shocks frequently pushes the natural rate of interest into negative territory,​
​increasing the frequency of ZLB episodes.​​1​

​3. The Physics of the Two-Asset HANK Model​
​The transition from a One-Asset to a Two-Asset framework is not merely an addition of a​
​variable; it fundamentally alters the household's optimization problem and the transmission​
​mechanism of aggregate shocks. The One-Asset model conflates the​
​consumption-smoothing motive with the wealth-accumulation motive, leading to​
​counterfactual implications for the MPC.​​1​ ​The Two-Asset​​model separates these motives by​
​introducing a liquid asset $b_t$ (government bonds) and an illiquid asset $a_t$ (capital or​
​equity).​

​3.1 The Household Budget Constraint and Transaction Costs​

​Following the formulation in Kaplan, Moll, and Violante (2018), the household faces a flow​
​budget constraint that accounts for the costs of rebalancing the portfolio. The crucial friction​
​is the transaction cost $\chi(d_t, a_t)$ associated with depositing or withdrawing funds from​
​the illiquid account.​​1​ ​The budget constraint is defined​​as:​

​$$c_t + b_{t+1} + q_t a_{t+1} = R_t^b b_t + (q_t + r_t^a) a_t + w_t e_t z_t - T_t - \chi(d_t, a_t)$$​
​Here, $b_t$ represents real liquid assets earning a real return $R_t^b / \pi_t$, and $a_t$​
​represents illiquid assets with a price $q_t$ and a dividend (or rental rate) $r_t^a$. The​
​variable $d_t$ denotes the flow of deposits into the illiquid account, defined as:​

​$$d_t = q_t a_{t+1} - (q_t + r_t^a) a_t$$​
​The transaction cost function $\chi(d_t, a_t)$ is typically modeled as a convex function that​
​exhibits an "inaction region" around $d_t = 0$. A common specification is:​

​$$\chi(d_t, a_t) = \chi_0 |d_t| + \chi_1 \left( \frac{d_t}{a_t} \right)^2 a_t$$​
​This function includes a linear component $\chi_0$, which generates a fixed cost of​

​adjustment (leading to inaction), and a quadratic component $\chi_1$, which penalizes large​
​rapid adjustments. This structure is essential for generating the "wealthy hand-to-mouth"​
​households—agents who hold substantial illiquid wealth but little liquid wealth and therefore​
​have high MPCs out of small transitory income shocks.​​1​

​3.2 Derivation of the Portfolio Euler Equation​

​The introduction of the illiquid asset creates a complex arbitrage condition that the neural​
​network must learn to satisfy. In the One-Asset model, the neural network minimizes the​
​residual of a single consumption Euler equation. In the Two-Asset model, the household​
​optimization yields two distinct first-order conditions: one for the liquid asset and one for the​
​illiquid asset.​
​The Euler equation for the liquid bond remains standard, governed by the borrowing​
​constraint $b_{t+1} \ge \underline{b}$:​

​$$u'(c_t) \ge \beta \mathbb{E}_t \left$$​
​The inequality holds strictly when the borrowing constraint binds.​
​The Euler equation for the illiquid asset is more complex due to the transaction cost. It​
​represents the arbitrage condition equating the marginal cost of saving in illiquid assets today​
​with the expected marginal benefit tomorrow. Differentiating the value function with respect​
​to $a_{t+1}$, we derive the Portfolio Euler Equation (adapted from Appendix B.2 of​​1​)​ :​
​$$ q_t \left( 1 + \frac{\partial \chi(d_t, a_t)}{\partial d_t} \right) u'(c_t) = \beta \mathbb{E}​t \left[​
​u'(c​{t+1}) \left( q_{t+1} + r_{t+1}^a + \frac{\partial​​\chi_{t+1}}{\partial a_{t+1}} - \frac{\partial​
​\chi_{t+1}}{\partial d_{t+1}} (q_{t+1} + r_{t+1}^a) \right) \right] $$​
​This equation encapsulates the core physics of the model. The term $\left( 1 + \frac{\partial​
​\chi}{\partial d_t} \right)$ represents the effective price of purchasing a unit of capital,​
​inclusive of the marginal transaction cost. The right-hand side captures the future payoff,​
​which includes the market return $(q_{t+1} + r_{t+1}^a)$ and the marginal reduction in future​
​transaction costs achieved by having a higher stock of illiquid assets (the term $\frac{\partial​
​\chi}{\partial a}$).​
​The neural network policy function $\psi_{NN}$ must output consumption $c_t$ and portfolio​
​deposit $d_t$ such that the residuals of both Euler equations are minimized. However, the​
​presence of the linear cost component $\chi_0 |d_t|$ introduces a non-differentiability (a​
​kink) at $d_t = 0$, where the derivative $\frac{\partial \chi}{\partial d_t}$ is undefined.​
​Standard backpropagation algorithms used to train neural networks rely on smooth gradients​

​and will fail to converge in the presence of such kinks. This necessitates the use of smoothing​
​techniques, specifically the Fischer-Burmeister transformation.​

​4. Handling Non-Convexities: The Math of Kinks​
​The "inaction region" generated by the transaction cost is a defining feature of the Two-Asset​
​model, creating a mass of households who do not adjust their capital holdings in response to​
​small shocks. This discrete choice (adjust vs. do not adjust) renders the optimization problem​
​non-convex. While traditional solution methods like value function iteration on a grid can​
​handle discrete choices via comparison operators (max), deep learning methods require​
​differentiable objective functions. To address this, we employ the methodology outlined by​
​Maliar, Maliar, and Winant (2021), using Fischer-Burmeister functions to smooth the​
​Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions.​​1​

​4.1 The Fischer-Burmeister Transformation​

​The Fischer-Burmeister (FB) function is a powerful tool for converting complementarity​
​problems into systems of smooth nonlinear equations. A complementarity problem is defined​
​by conditions of the form:​

​$$x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0, \quad x \cdot y = 0$$​
​The FB function $\Phi(x, y)$ serves as a smooth equivalent to these conditions:​

​$$\Phi(x, y) = x + y - \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = 0$$​
​This function is zero if and only if $x$ and $y$ satisfy the complementarity conditions. It is​
​differentiable everywhere except at the origin $(0,0)$, and even there, it has well-defined​
​directional derivatives that allow gradient-based optimizers to proceed.​

​4.2 Smoothing the Borrowing Constraint​

​The borrowing constraint $b_{t+1} \ge \underline{b}$ creates a kink in the liquid asset policy​

​function. Let $\lambda_t$ be the Lagrange multiplier on the borrowing constraint. The KKT​
​conditions imply:​

​$$\lambda_t \ge 0, \quad b_{t+1} - \underline{b} \ge 0, \quad \lambda_t (b_{t+1} -​
​\underline{b}) = 0$$​
​We can define the Euler residual for the unconstrained case as $h_t = u'(c_t) - \beta​
​\mathbb{E}_t$. In a constrained optimum, $h_t = \lambda_t$. Thus, we can rewrite the​
​constrained Euler equation using the FB function:​
​$$ \mathcal{L}​{borrow} = \Phi(h_t, b​{t+1} - \underline{b})​​= h_t + (b_{t+1} - \underline{b}) -​
​\sqrt{h_t^2 + (b_{t+1} - \underline{b})^2} = 0 $$​
​Minimizing the squared error of $\mathcal{L}_{borrow}$ allows the neural network to learn the​
​policy function for both constrained and unconstrained agents within a single smooth loss​
​landscape.​​1​

​4.3 Smoothing the Portfolio Adjustment Kink​

​The portfolio adjustment decision presents a more complex "double kink." The household​
​adjusts capital only if the marginal gain from adjusting exceeds the marginal cost. Let $\mu_t$​
​be the shadow value of adjusting the portfolio. The optimality conditions imply an inaction​
​band: if the shadow value is within $[-\chi^-, +\chi^+]$, then $d_t = 0$.​
​To enable neural network training, we smooth the transaction cost function $\chi(d, a)$ itself.​
​Instead of the non-differentiable absolute value function $|d|$, we can employ a smooth​
​approximation such as the pseudo-Huber loss function or a hyperbola approximation:​

​$$|d| \approx \sqrt{d^2 + \epsilon^2} - \epsilon$$​
​where $\epsilon$ is a small smoothing parameter. This ensures that the marginal cost​
​$\frac{\partial \chi}{\partial d}$ is continuous and differentiable at $d=0$, allowing gradients​
​to flow through the inaction point. Alternatively, one can apply the FB function to the upper​
​and lower bounds of the inaction inequalities, effectively replacing the discrete regime​
​switching logic with a continuous "degree of bindingness." This approach is essential for the​
​KMR optimizer to navigate the parameter space without getting stuck in local minima caused​
​by discontinuous gradients.​

​5. State Space Expansion and High Dimensionality​
​Extending the KMR framework to the Two-Asset model precipitates a dramatic expansion of​
​the state space. The distribution of households is no longer defined over a 2D domain $(b, e)$​
​but a 3D domain $(b, a, e)$, leading to the "curse of dimensionality."​

​5.1 The Neural Network Input Vector​

​To capture the aggregate state of the economy, the neural network input vector must be​
​augmented. In the baseline KMR implementation, the aggregate state is often approximated​
​by tracking the histories of a finite set of simulated agents or by using a set of moments. The​
​Two-Asset extension requires the following specific additions to the input vector​
​$\mathbb{S}_t$:​
​1.​ ​Idiosyncratic States:​​The input layer must accept​​the triplet $(b_{it}, a_{it}, e_{it})$ for​
​the set of simulated agents. The inclusion of $a_{it}$ (illiquid assets) is non-negotiable,​
​as the distribution of illiquid wealth is the primary driver of the investment response to​
​shocks.​
​2.​ ​Aggregate States:​​Beyond the standard productivity​​($Z_t$) and monetary ($R_t$)​
​states, the input must include the aggregate capital stock $K_t = \int a_{it} \, d\mu_t$.​
​Furthermore, if adjustment costs depend on aggregate investment (as in models with​
​capital adjustment costs), the lagged aggregate investment $I_{t-1}$ must also be​
​included.​
​3.​ ​Structural Parameters (Pseudo-States):​​To enable the​​estimation of the Two-Asset​
​specific frictions, the parameter vector $\Theta$ included in the input must be expanded​
​to include:​
​○​ ​$\chi_0, \chi_1$: The scaling parameters of the portfolio adjustment cost.​
​○​ ​$\sigma_{\chi}$: If adjustment costs are stochastic, their volatility.​
​○​ ​$\xi$: The elasticity of substitution between assets (if applicable).​

​5.2 The "Deep Learning Proof": Single Network Architecture​

​A critical theoretical question is whether a single neural network can effectively approximate​
​the policy functions for such a high-dimensional system, or if separate networks are required​
​for each asset decision. The research by Han et al. (2021) on the "DeepHAM" framework​

​provides the theoretical justification for using a single, unified network architecture.​​1​
​Han et al. demonstrate that deep neural networks possess an inherent capability for​
​"automatic model reduction." When trained on high-dimensional inputs representing a​
​distribution (e.g., a sample of thousands of agents), the hidden layers of the network learn to​
​extract the relevant low-dimensional features (or "generalized moments") that drive the​
​aggregate dynamics. This effectively replicates the logic of the Krusell-Smith​
​algorithm—where the infinite distribution is approximated by a few moments—but does so​
​endogenously and non-linearly.​
​This implies that we do not need to manually engineer a set of moments (e.g., mean liquid​
​wealth, share of hand-to-mouth households) to feed into the network. Instead, we can feed a​
​random sample of agent states $(b_i, a_i, e_i)$ into a permutation-invariant layer (such as a​
​"Set Transformer" or a simple mean-pooling layer) at the beginning of the network. The​
​network will learn the sufficient statistics of the distribution required to forecast prices $q_t$​
​and $w_t$. This "Deep Learning Proof" confirms that a single policy network​
​$\pi(\mathbb{S}_t, \Theta)$ outputting the vector $(c_t, b_{t+1}, a_{t+1})$ is not only feasible​
​but likely more stable than training disjoint networks for each decision margin.​

​5.3 The "All-in-One" Expectation Operator​

​A major computational cost in training these networks is the evaluation of expectations​
​integrals, $\mathbb{E}_t [\cdot]$, which appear in the Euler equations. Standard quadrature​
​methods suffer exponentially from the curse of dimensionality as the number of shocks​
​increases. Maliar, Maliar, and Winant (2021) introduce the "All-in-One" (AiO) expectation​
​operator to address this.​​1​
​The AiO method merges the optimization step with the integration step. Instead of evaluating​
​the expectation integral accurately at every training step (which would require thousands of​
​draws), the AiO method uses two independent random draws of the future shocks​
​$\epsilon_{t+1}$ and $\epsilon'_{t+1}$ to construct an unbiased estimator of the squared​
​residual.​
​Specifically, for an Euler residual function $\mathcal{R}(s, \epsilon)$, instead of minimizing​
​$(\mathbb{E})^2$, the AiO approach minimizes $\mathcal{R}(s, \epsilon) \cdot \mathcal{R}(s,​
​\epsilon')$. This reduces the cost of integration from $N_{quadrature}$ to just 2 evaluations​
​per training step. Over the course of thousands of training iterations (epochs), the stochastic​
​gradient descent algorithm averages out the noise, converging to the true expectation.​
​Implementing the AiO operator in the KMR extension is critical for maintaining computational​

​feasibility when adding the extra dimensions of the Two-Asset model.​

​6. Bayesian Identification Strategy and Priors​
​The ultimate goal of extending the KMR framework is to estimate the model on US data. This​
​requires a rigorous identification strategy. The Two-Asset model introduces new parameters​
​that are often weakly identified by standard aggregate time series (GDP, inflation). To ensure​
​robust estimation, we must utilize specific priors derived from the HANK literature and target​
​specific moments that identify the portfolio friction.​

​6.2 Identification Strategy: The "Investment Wedge"​

​A key insight from Luetticke (2025) is that aggregate uncertainty shocks in a Two-Asset model​
​generate a "flight to safety" that is distinct from standard precautionary savings.​​1​ ​When​
​uncertainty regarding Total Factor Productivity (TFP) rises, households not only consume less​
​(standard Euler effect) but also actively rebalance their portfolios away from the risky illiquid​
​asset toward the safe liquid asset. This generates an "investment wedge"—a divergence​
​between the return on capital and the risk-free rate that cannot be explained by standard​
​adjustment costs alone.​
​To capture this mechanism, the estimation strategy must include parameters governing the​
​volatility of volatility​​(stochastic volatility of​​TFP). The KMR neural network is uniquely suited​
​to identify this nonlinear mechanism because it solves the global nonlinear solution.​
​Linearized estimators (like standard Kalman filters) typically treat uncertainty shocks as level​
​shocks to the discount factor, missing the second-order portfolio rebalancing effects. By​
​treating the volatility state $\sigma_{Z,t}$ as an explicit input to the neural network, the​
​estimator can capture the state-dependent sensitivity of investment to uncertainty, providing​
​a powerful source of identification for the adjustment cost parameter $\chi_1$.​

​7. Feasibility, Stability, and Robustness​
​The implementation of this advanced estimator faces several stability challenges​
​well-documented in the literature. Addressing these​​ex-ante​​is essential for the convergence​
​of the KMR optimization loop.​

​7.1 The ZLB Instability and Shadow Rates​

​Acharya et al. (2023) emphasize that estimating HANK models on real data is fraught with​
​instability due to the Zero Lower Bound.​​1​ ​In a Two-Asset​​model, the "flight to safety" puts​
​immense downward pressure on the natural rate of interest. If the model gets stuck at the ZLB​
​for long simulation periods, the gradients used to update the neural network weights may​
​vanish or explode, leading to a "degenerate" solution where the estimator fails to learn the​
​dynamics.​
​To mitigate this, the loss function in the KMR code should incorporate a​​Shadow Rate​

​penalty​​or use a smooth approximation of the ZLB kink. Instead of modeling the nominal rate​
​as $R_t = \max(1, R_t^*)$, we employ a Softplus function:​

​$$R_t = 1 + \frac{1}{\kappa} \log(1 + e^{\kappa (R_t^* - 1)})$$​
​This function approaches the max operator as $\kappa \to \infty$ but remains strictly​
​differentiable everywhere. This ensures that non-zero gradients propagate back through the​
​network even when the economy is deep in a liquidity trap, allowing the optimizer to navigate​
​out of the ZLB region during training.​

​7.2 The "Volatility Paradox"​

​Reiter (2019) identifies a "Volatility Paradox" in linearized heterogeneous agent models:​
​linearization kills the precautionary savings channel because volatility is inherently a​
​second-moment phenomenon.​​1​ ​A linear approximation​​around a deterministic steady state​
​sets variance terms to zero.​
​The KMR framework, being a​​global nonlinear solution​,​​is immune to this paradox in theory.​
​However, feasibility depends on the network capacity. The neural network must be sufficiently​
​wide (e.g., 256+ neurons per layer) to capture the curvature of the policy function with​
​respect to the volatility state. A "thin" network will essentially perform a linear regression,​
​reproducing the volatility paradox. Therefore, the architecture must be over-parameterized​
​relative to standard solution networks to capture these higher-order moments.​

​7.3 The "Missing Middle" and Distributional Constraints​

​Kaplan and Violante (2022) warn of the "Missing Middle" problem in One-Asset models, where​
​the wealth distribution becomes polarized into rich and poor with no middle class.​​1​ ​Two-Asset​
​models can solve this, but only if the return gap between liquid and illiquid assets is calibrated​
​precisely. If the estimated adjustment cost $\chi_1$ drifts too low, the two assets become​
​perfect substitutes, and the model collapses back to the One-Asset logic, resurrecting the​
​"Missing Middle" and degrading the fit to aggregate consumption data.​
​To prevent this regime collapse during estimation, the loss function should include a​
​regularization term that penalizes deviations from key distributional moments. Specifically,​
​adding a penalty for the​​Liquid Wealth Share​​(targeting​​approx. 5% of total wealth,​
​consistent with US data) ensures that the estimator respects the segmentation of the asset​

​markets. This acts as a "soft restriction" that guides the neural network toward the​
​economically relevant region of the parameter space where the Two-Asset frictions are active.​

​8. Implementation Roadmap​
​The execution of this research project follows a structured roadmap designed to​
​incrementally build complexity and verify stability at each stage.​
​Phase 1: Data Preparation and Moment Matching​
​●​ ​Aggregate US time series for Real GDP, Inflation (PCE), and the Federal Funds Rate are​
​compiled.​
​●​ ​Crucially, distributional moments are computed from the Survey of Consumer Finances​
​(SCF) to serve as validation targets: the Gini coefficients for liquid and illiquid wealth, and​
​the share of hand-to-mouth households.​
​Phase 2: Network Architecture Expansion​
​●​ ​The NKModel class in the KMR codebase is modified. The input dimension is increased to​
​accept the distribution of agents $(b, a, e)$ and the vector of new parameters​
​$\Theta_{new}$.​
​●​ ​The "Set Transformer" layer is implemented to process the agent distribution in a​
